# The price of a turn

## 1. Skiing’s carbon footprint is lost to history. Snowboarding’s could still be counted.

At a ski resort in Altay, a day’s skiing begins when the lift opens. A few valleys away, that would be a curious definition of morning. Herders in the Altay mountains have long used wooden skis lined with horsehide to get about in winter. Suppose a herder skis out to find a stray mare and rather enjoys the descent home. How much of the trip should a carbon accountant enter under sport?

Skiing had a long career before anyone thought to sell admission. On Norway’s Mount Digervarden, the ice preserved a pair of skis some 1,300 years old. An archaeologist can tell you what the wood was. No examination of the grain will tell you how many journeys the owner made, what each was for, or how far he would have walked without them. Start the ledger with the first resort and centuries of skiing disappear. Start with the first skier and even the opening date is anybody’s guess.

Modern snowboarding offers the auditor the rare pleasure of a Christmas with paperwork. On Christmas Day in 1965, Sherman Poppen fastened two children’s skis together in Michigan to amuse his daughters. His wife, Nancy, supplied the name *Snurfer*. A year later he licensed it to Brunswick. One may debate who first stood sideways on snow. For the commercial sport that followed, there is a prototype in the Smithsonian and a company whose books one might reasonably ask to see.

From there an industrious researcher could trace board production, estimate materials and freight, and use participation records to apportion journeys and lift power. Backyard afternoons would escape the count. Some early invoices will have gone missing. Still, a range with an allowance for missing evidence is a figure worth publishing. The same arithmetic works for a skier taking the chair in Altay this morning. It simply cannot follow that skier all the way back through history.

Snowboarding thus has the better claim to a lifetime carbon account. Its first entry would make a fine initiation for a junior auditor. On Christmas morning in Michigan, which sport gets charged for the two skis?

## 2. Hey, snowboarders. Flatten the black diamond in one go and we’ll have to ban that.

The skier at the lip of a black diamond has a precise grievance. There was a lovely pocket of soft snow below the entrance. A snowboarder has just descended sideways through the lot. The pocket is now several yards downhill, where nobody particularly wanted it. The mountain retains its gradient. This is scant consolation.

Fresh snow on a narrow pitch is a peculiar part of the lift ticket. The resort can sell the same view to a thousand people. It can sell first tracks exactly once. The first rider has paid to enjoy the snow and owes the second no particular pattern of turns. But someone who returns expressly to see how much of the entrance he can strip in one slide is getting a private joke subsidised by everybody behind him. A patroller is entitled to spoil the fun.

The difficulty comes when the next rider looks over that lip and discovers she has made a dreadful booking error. A controlled sideslip may be her safest way down. Put up a sign threatening to confiscate her pass and she has a new reason to attempt a turn she cannot manage. The skier waiting above her also has an obligation. Under the American ski areas’ responsibility code, the person downhill has priority.

Give patrol discretion to warn a rider who deliberately scours a bottleneck and to bar a repeat offender from that run for the day. Apply the rule equally to the skier who comes back to spray the same entrance with a theatrical hockey stop. As for the woman edging gingerly towards easier ground, let her finish. She has already received a fairly searching review of her technique from the chairlift.

## 3. Would private snow-surfing slopes be a good incentive for snowboarders?

Consider a snowboard school with an unusual offer. For an hour on Saturday morning, its pupils may book a snow lane shaped like a succession of small breaking waves. A child can ride up one bank, come down the other side and try again without an expert overtaking halfway through the turn. Her father, watching from the fence, sees something worth buying next weekend. That is a respectable commercial argument for a little privacy.

There is evidence that the shape itself has appeal. At Laax in Switzerland, organisers of the 2026 banked slalom listed 300 riders, aged five to 65, for a course with seventeen and a half turns. A race entry establishes a taste for banked snow. It tells a resort rather less about how many beginners would pay extra for it on a wet Tuesday in March.

Who supplies the slope matters as much as who pays for the gate. If a club finances the shaping of an underused patch and sells sessions that cover upkeep, other passholders may get a quieter morning at somebody else’s expense. If management ropes off the only gentle return to the village, the very same receipts tell a different story. A family may pay the supplement simply to get home. The accounts will duly record an enthusiasm for surfing.

Try the lane for a season. Include a first session with a beginner’s lesson, charge clubs for exclusive hours and leave the ordinary route home open. Then ask how many first-timers came back to ride at their own expense, and what happened to queues elsewhere. A full Saturday of regulars would make a decent case for a club. The child persuading her father to drive up on a damp Tuesday would make a case for the sport.

## 4. Skiing and its resorts versus the First World War. Which left the larger carbon footprint?

In October 1915, Italian soldiers in the Dolomites spotted Austrian troops practising on skis and sledges. The Italian army diary records the exercise because Italian fire interrupted it. A historian of skiing and a historian of the First World War could quite properly cite the same entry. A carbon accountant who added their two columns would have a problem.

Those military skiers contributed to the war’s emissions. Somebody had to make their equipment and get it to the mountain. The fuel attributable to that work belongs in an account of wartime skiing, even though gravity supplied the downhill ride. It also belongs in an account of military procurement. In a combined total it belongs there once. The diary establishes the skiing. It offers no tonnage, and gives us no reason to charge an entire Alpine garrison’s winter fuel to the men on skis.

A little arithmetic shows why the larger question is worth asking. Historian Warwick Michael Brown estimated that Britain’s Grand Fleet burned between 5.5m and 7.5m long tons of coal during the war. Using a modern American figure for bituminous coal as a rough stand-in for the fleet’s fuel gives **about 14m–20m metric tonnes of CO₂** from combustion alone. That buys the reader one fleet, under one flag, burning one fuel. The armies, merchant shipping and munitions factories have yet to submit their accounts.

Now take one winter. The ski industry reported more than 366m resort visits worldwide in 2023/24. A French study put a visitor-day at three resorts at 48.9kg of CO₂-equivalent, including travel, accommodation, equipment and resort services. If 366m visits had each resembled the French average, the season would have come to **about 18m tonnes of CO₂-equivalent**. Applying a French holiday to the entire planet is a large assumption. A family arriving by rail and one flying across an ocean give some idea why. Adding the resorts’ emissions again would create a second error.

The striking result is how little of the war it takes to reach the scale of that illustrative season. The whole war very likely exceeded one such winter. **The evidence does not settle the contest between the war and the cumulative history of skiing and its resorts.** For that, we would need successive decades of journeys, buildings and visitor numbers, as well as an estimate of wartime production across the belligerents. An Alpine herder’s journey centuries before the first chairlift can hardly be priced as a French resort holiday.

### Is the comparison ethical?

Yes, if it helps us see a cost that each institution has reason to overlook. A resort can point to an electric chairlift while its customers fly in. A military ledger can record coal bought and leave the smoke to posterity. Asking the same physical question of both is fair. Assigning responsibility takes a second question. A conscript sent up a mountain, the government that sent him and a holidaymaker choosing a flight had very different powers of decision.

There is a revealing test. Suppose a blockade closes civilian factories and their emissions fall. A study of the war’s effect on the atmosphere ought to record that fall against a plausible peacetime course. Imagine offering the resulting carbon saving to a family deprived of its livelihood as evidence that the blockade did them some good. The cruelty of the proposition survives impeccable arithmetic. Count the coal. Ask who had a choice. And if the figures persuade a resort to run a train connection next winter, that is a better use for them than deciding who won the last century.
