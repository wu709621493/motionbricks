# Enamel Genetics and Melanin Turnover in Spaceflight Calcium Research

Evidence synthesis and a testable research framework

Expanded edition including allicin and capsaicin exposure and age related epigenetic regulation

Prepared for Windy Wu  |  2 November 2026

### Purpose and main finding

This document integrates genetic causes of defective dental enamel, the biochemical formation and turnover of eumelanin and pheomelanin, and a proposed connection to calcium regulation during spaceflight. It separates established mechanisms from hypotheses that require new measurements. It is a research discussion, not a diagnosis of an individual dental condition.

Pathogenic variants can cause enamel to develop with inadequate thickness, mineralization or hardness. Melanin synthesis and melanosomal ion regulation are also experimentally established. However, no direct evidence identified in this targeted review shows that pheomelanin production increases whole-body calcium requirements, causes enamel softening, or explains astronaut bone loss.

### Origin of the proposal

The proposal to examine eumelanin and pheomelanin turnover in the context of astronaut calcium cycling was raised by Windy Wu. The evidence organization, quantitative qualifications and staged experimental framework below were developed with AI assistance. Proposed mechanisms are not represented as observed results.

### Evidence boundaries

| Established evidence | Open question |
| --- | --- |
| Certain enamel-development genes cause amelogenesis imperfecta. | Does any pigment-related pathway modify adult oral mineral balance? |
| Both pigments undergo chemical changes; eumelanin can bind calcium. | Do pigment composition and turnover alter a physiologically meaningful calcium flux? |
| Spaceflight can alter bone turnover, calcium absorption and excretion. | Does pigment biology add explanatory value beyond known flight-related factors? |

Evidence search scope: selected primary biochemical, genetic and human spaceflight studies, with official enzyme and dental references. This is not a systematic review; absence of an identified study is not proof that no study exists. Numbered references provide source links.

## 1 Genetic causes of defective enamel

Amelogenesis imperfecta (AI) comprises inherited developmental abnormalities of enamel. Hypoplasia means reduced enamel quantity; hypomaturation and hypocalcification describe deficient quality or hardening. These differ from post-eruptive acid demineralization and caries, although both can coexist. A report of multiple cavities alone does not diagnose AI. [1, 2]

| Gene | Mechanism and phenotype | Typical inheritance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AMELX | Amelogenin matrix abnormalities; thin and/or hypomature enamel. [3] | X-linked |
| ENAM | Enamelin defects affecting enamel formation; pits or generalized thin enamel. [4] | Dominant or recessive |
| MMP20 | Early matrix proteolysis defects; hypomaturation with possible hypoplasia. [5] | Usually recessive |
| KLK4 | Deficient matrix-protein removal during maturation; poor mineralization. [5] | Usually recessive |
| FAM83H | Specific truncating variants cause hypocalcified AI with deficient hardness. [6] | Usually dominant |
| SLC24A4 | Potassium-dependent sodium/calcium exchanger; impaired maturation-stage ion transport. [7] | Typically recessive |
| WDR72 | Maturation-related trafficking defects; enamel may retain normal thickness but be poorly mineralized. [8] | Usually recessive |
| FAM20A | Severe hypoplasia, eruption abnormalities and possible enamel–renal syndrome. [9] | Recessive |

Representative reported variants include FAM83H p.Arg325* and p.Gln398*, MMP20 p.His204Arg, and KLK4 p.Gly82Alafs*87. These are literature examples, not a complete diagnostic panel; clinical reporting requires an explicit transcript and full variant classification. [5, 6]

WDR72-related AI can coexist with renal tubular acidosis; FAM20A-related disease can include nephrocalcinosis. These observations illustrate that defective enamel does not necessarily mean insufficient dietary calcium. They do not justify screening every person with caries for renal disease. [8, 9]

## 2 Interpreting the genetic overlap

### Shared genes do not establish competition for calcium

SLC24A4 is relevant because common variation near this locus is associated with pigmentation, whereas certain damaging variants cause enamel maturation defects. Association with hair or eye color and causation of AI are different claims involving different variants and evidence. Neither establishes that pigment synthesis withdraws calcium from enamel. [7, 10]

For a genetic result to be clinically explanatory, the exact variant, zygosity, inheritance pattern, segregation and dental phenotype must agree. A variant of uncertain significance is not a confirmed diagnosis. The core genes listed here are not an exhaustive catalogue of isolated or syndromic enamel disorders.

### Clinical observations that distinguish mechanisms

The first distinction is whether softness refers to carious dentin within a cavity, or to broadly abnormal enamel in regions without caries. Developmental clues include abnormalities present when teeth erupted, generalized or patterned defects, early wear or fracture, similar primary and permanent tooth findings, and a compatible family history. Lack of family history does not exclude recessive or newly arising disease. [1]

Dental examination and indicated radiographs should document each tooth, lesion depth, enamel thickness and mineralization appearance. Developmental AI, acquired demineralization and other dental abnormalities should be considered before selecting genetic testing. Existing enamel defects and active caries still require appropriate dental care regardless of whether a genetic cause is found.

### Enamel is not a miniature remodeling bone

The spaceflight comparison must distinguish actively remodeling bone from erupted enamel. Adult enamel does not participate in osteoclast–osteoblast remodeling. Oral enamel mineral exchange depends on local conditions, including acid exposure, saliva and fluoride; early demineralization can sometimes be arrested or remineralized, while established cavities require repair. Thus, astronaut skeletal calcium loss cannot be equated directly with adult enamel softening. [2]

### Current causal conclusion

The sequence “more pheomelanin → greater calcium consumption → systemic calcium deficiency → soft enamel” remains unverified. A shared transporter is a reason to design compartment-specific experiments, not evidence for this entire sequence. Hair color, eye color and ancestry should not substitute for pigment chemistry, calcium measurements or clinical diagnosis.

## 3 Formation and turnover of the two pigments

### The shared enzymatic entry

Both pathways use dopaquinone (DQ), generated through tyrosinase activity. The familiar schematic is L-tyrosine to L-DOPA to DQ. The net monophenolase reaction converts L-tyrosine and molecular oxygen to DQ and water; the scheme does not require free DOPA to be released and rebound for every turnover. Tyrosinase is a copper-containing catalyst. This net reaction does not directly hydrolyze ATP, but maintaining the cells, enzymes and organelle environment carries broader metabolic costs. [11]

### Eumelanin formation

When thiol capture is limited, DQ undergoes intramolecular cyclization to cyclodopa, also called leucodopachrome. Cyclodopa reacts with another DQ molecule through redox exchange, forming dopachrome while reducing DQ to DOPA. Dopachrome then yields DHI or, through the DCT-associated route, DHICA. Subsequent oxidation and coupling produce heterogeneous indolic pigment structures. There is no single universal eumelanin molecular formula, chain length or oxidation state. [12, 13]

### Pheomelanin formation

Available cysteine captures DQ to generate cysteinyldopa isomers, principally 5-S-cysteinyldopa with additional isomers including 2-S-cysteinyldopa. Oxidation yields cysteinyldopa quinones, followed by cyclization, rearrangement and, in some routes, decarboxylation. Benzothiazine-related intermediates participate in subsequent pigment formation; later changes can generate benzothiazole-related structures. These are heterogeneous reaction networks rather than a single linear polymerization with a fixed endpoint. [14–16]

MFSD12 is required for normal cysteine import into melanosomes and supports the production of cysteinyldopa precursors. This is direct evidence for a local substrate-supply mechanism, not for greater intestinal calcium absorption. Rapid cysteine capture does not guarantee rapid formation of mature pheomelanin because downstream oxidation can remain limiting. [14, 17]

### What turnover means

Synthesis, transfer between cells, organelle processing, chemical oxidation and loss with tissue renewal must be measured separately. UVA can alter both eumelanin and pheomelanin. Benzothiazine-to-benzothiazole conversion is not equivalent to complete pigment destruction and resynthesis. Nor does one absorbed photon imply consumption of one pigment molecule. A synthesis rate, standing pigment mass and degradation rate are distinct quantities. [16, 18]

## 4 Quantitative results and their limits

### Photon accounting

At a single wavelength of 300 nm, a photon carries 6.62 × 10⁻¹⁹ joule. Absorption of 1 mJ therefore corresponds to approximately 1.51 × 10¹⁵ single-photon absorption events. At 365 nm, the corresponding count is 1.84 × 10¹⁵. These are calculations from photon energy, not measurements of pigment demand.

A pigment can dissipate energy and absorb again. Particle number additionally requires a particle definition, absorption cross-section, geometry, intensity, exposure duration and damage or recovery behavior. Ultrafast energy dissipation has been observed in eumelanin, and absorption alone is not a measure of biological protection. [19]

### Local reaction kinetics

| Reaction | Reported rate constant | Calculated half-time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DQ cyclization to cyclodopa | 3.8 s⁻¹ | 0.182 s |
| Cyclodopa oxidation by DQ | 5.3 × 10⁶ M⁻¹ s⁻¹ | 0.131 s at fixed 1 μM DQ |
| 5-S-cysteinyldopa oxidation by DQ | 8.8 × 10⁵ M⁻¹ s⁻¹ | 0.788 s at fixed 1 μM DQ |

Calculations use ln(2) divided by the first-order rate constant, or by the second-order constant multiplied by a constant co-reactant concentration. The DQ concentration is an illustrative assumption, not a measured melanosomal concentration. The published constants are retained without extrapolation to 37 °C. The last two entries are conditional pseudo-first-order calculations, not half-times in a closed, depleting reaction mixture. [12, 14]

The approximately sixfold difference applies only to these redox-exchange constants. It cannot be assigned to the overall synthesis pathways. Individual half-times cannot simply be added to obtain a pathway completion time.

### Thermodynamics and resource costs

Reaction Gibbs energy depends on the standard reaction Gibbs energy plus a concentration-dependent term, RT ln Q; a fixed-pH biochemical treatment requires consistently transformed quantities. At 310.15 K, a tenfold increase in the dimensionless reaction quotient adds 5.94 kJ/mol. This is only a concentration correction.

A full pathway value requires defined pigment structures, oxidation states, balanced reactions, thermodynamic data and activities. Forward rate constants alone do not provide reaction Gibbs energies. Activation barriers are not ATP costs and must not be summed as a biosynthetic energy bill. No defensible total synthesis time, total Gibbs energy or calcium cost per mJ is established here.

## 5 Discussion of calcium cycling in spaceflight

### The established aerospace biology problem

Calcium homeostasis is a longstanding priority in human spaceflight biology. Mechanical unloading changes bone remodeling; flight studies have documented increased bone resorption, altered intestinal absorption and increased urinary calcium. These processes concern flux between diet, intestine, circulation, skeleton and excretion, not simply low blood calcium. [20]

A historical stable-isotope study in three male space travelers reported lower calcium intake and absorption, greater resorption and urinary excretion, and estimated loss of about 250 mg bone calcium per day during flight. Its small cohort, mission conditions and countermeasures limit generalization. Modern resistance exercise and nutritional support can attenuate bone-density deficits, yet a study of 17 astronauts found incomplete recovery of several tibial outcomes one year after return. [20–22]

### A plausible cellular connection

Melanin can bind calcium. Direct biochemical work on Sepia eumelanin supports investigation of melanosomes as local calcium-buffering compartments. This evidence strengthens the rationale for studying pigment–ion interactions but does not establish a greater calcium requirement for pheomelanin, a human whole-body calcium sink, or a flight-specific effect. [23]

The proposed hypothesis is that spaceflight-related conditions may alter pigment synthesis, structural conversion or turnover, which in turn may modify local calcium binding, release, signaling or redox state. A separate and stronger hypothesis is that these local effects measurably influence systemic calcium balance. The first can be tested without presuming the second.

### Three mechanisms that must remain separate

First, calcium signaling may regulate melanocyte behavior without substantial net calcium consumption. Second, pigment binding may redistribute or temporarily store calcium without removing it from the body. Third, pigment loss with tissue shedding could export bound calcium, but its magnitude would need direct mass-balance measurement. Chemical calcium binding is not itself a biosynthetic requirement.

### Exposure and tissue boundaries

Microgravity, solar ultraviolet exposure and ionizing space radiation are distinct experimental variables. UV pigment-absorption calculations do not predict protection against high-energy particles. Any flight application must use measured cabin, window or extravehicular exposure conditions rather than assume unshielded skin exposure. A ground rotating culture also introduces fluid and shear effects and is not equivalent to orbital microgravity.

Adult dental endpoints should be evaluated through saliva, local mineral chemistry, acid exposure and pre-existing enamel defects. Developmental gene defects in ameloblasts should not be treated as evidence of active remodeling of adult enamel during a mission. The proposed pigment research should complement established exercise, nutrition and clinical monitoring rather than displace them.

## 6 A staged test of the pigment turnover hypothesis

The following is a proposed framework, not an approved protocol or a report of results. The initial objective is to determine whether pigment-dependent calcium flux exists and is large enough to justify a human spaceflight study.

| Stage | Measurements and controls | Decision criterion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Pigment chemistry | Matched, chemically characterized pigments; calcium binding and release, pH, ionic strength, competing metals, particle size and surface area. | Reproducible compositional effect beyond surface area and nonspecific adsorption. |
| 2 Human cell models | Melanocyte–keratinocyte cultures; pigment synthesis and loss, free and total calcium, viability, redox markers; matched genetic backgrounds and rescue controls. | A causal local effect without cytotoxicity or probe interference. |
| 3 Spaceflight analogs | Factorial gravity-analog and radiation conditions; sham rotation, fluid/shear controls and separated UV versus ionizing exposures. | A replicated interaction that survives appropriate physical controls. |
| 4 Human observational study | Repeated calcium balance, bone and pigment measurements alongside diet, exercise and mission exposure records. | Added predictive value beyond known factors, with uncertainty quantified. |

### Measurements that make the hypothesis falsifiable

Pigment composition should be measured chemically with validated degradation-marker assays and recovery controls, rather than inferred from hair color or ancestry. Stable-isotope amino-acid pulse–chase approaches could estimate synthesis and loss after validation. Pigment optical properties can interfere with fluorescent calcium or redox probes, so orthogonal assays are necessary. Proposed endpoints include calcium-binding capacity, release kinetics, intracellular free calcium and total cellular calcium.

A human extension would pair clinically supervised calcium absorption or balance methods with urinary calcium, renal function, PTH, vitamin D status, bone resorption and formation markers, and indicated skeletal imaging. Oral measures could include salivary flow, pH and calcium/phosphate, with baseline enamel defects documented separately. Whole-body interpretation requires intake, excretion and redistribution; serum calcium alone is insufficient.

### Mass balance and a stopping rule

For a shedding mechanism, estimate daily calcium export as pigment mass lost per day multiplied by calcium bound per unit pigment mass, counting only material actually exported. For storage, measure change in calcium-bound pigment inventory and subsequent release. Recycling within the body must not be counted as loss. Compare the resulting confidence interval with independently measured skeletal and renal fluxes; do not assume a meaningful effect size in advance.

If local effects are absent, fail rescue controls, or remain too small to contribute materially to systemic calcium flux, reject or narrow the systemic hypothesis. Small astronaut cohorts require prespecified outcomes, repeated within-person measurements and independent validation. Pigmentation or ancestry should not be used for crew selection, supplementation decisions or disease prediction on the basis of this hypothesis.

## 7 Dietary compounds and oral microbial effects

Allicin and capsaicin are added as separate, measured exposure variables. Dietary intake, local oral contact and systemic tissue exposure are not interchangeable. Record food preparation, formulation, frequency, contact duration, active compound stability and measured saliva or plasma exposure where feasible. Garlic mass is not an allicin dose; pungency is not a capsaicin concentration.

### Allicin and thiol chemistry

Allicin can modify protein cysteines through S-thioallylation and perturb low-molecular-weight thiol pools. This has been demonstrated in bacterial and human cell models. It motivates testing cysteine availability, glutathione status and pigment synthesis together, but it does not show that eating garlic shifts the eumelanin–pheomelanin ratio. Jurkat-cell findings cannot be assigned directly to melanocytes. Allicin sulfur is not equivalent to delivery of free cysteine to melanosomes. [24, 25]

### Capsaicin and melanocyte signaling

One cultured-melanocyte study reported reduced melanin and melanogenic protein levels after 50 μM capsaicin exposure, with a proposed TRPV1–ERK–MITF mechanism. This is evidence for an experimental cellular effect, not proof of an oral dietary effect, increased systemic calcium consumption, or a selective reduction in either pigment class. TRPV1-dependent calcium signaling should be assessed directly with viability, antagonist or genetic controls, and pigment-specific chemical assays. [26]

| Exposure and model | Reported result | Interpretation limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purified allicin and oral pathogens | Planktonic S. mutans inhibition at 600 μg/mL or above; mature-biofilm killing decreased with depth. [27] | Not a dietary dose or a validated mouth-rinse concentration. |
| Capsaicin in S. mutans–C. albicans coculture | MIC 320 μg/mL; 160–320 μg/mL reduced selected biofilm, acid and EPS outcomes. [28] | Two-species in vitro model; Candida is a fungus, not a bacterium. |
| Garlic extract on orthodontic wire | Growth inhibition coexisted with increased S. mutans attachment. [29] | Extract composition and substrate can change the outcome. |

MIC values are assay-specific and cannot rank these compounds across different organisms and media. Killing selected organisms is not the same as improving plaque-community ecology or preventing caries. Include multispecies communities, commensal recovery, viable counts, acid production, extracellular polysaccharides and enamel mineral-loss endpoints; total biomass alone is insufficient.

## 8 Exposure bridges and age related regulation

### From ingestion to relevant tissue concentration

Human garlic studies show that processing, formulation and meal conditions affect metabolite-based estimates of allicin bioavailability. These estimates are not proof that intact allicin reaches melanocytes or plaque at an inhibitory concentration. Human capsaicin pharmacokinetics likewise cannot be replaced by a nominal culture concentration. Initial research should characterize exposure before choosing mechanistic doses. [30, 31]

Keep oral antimicrobial and systemic pigment hypotheses separate. A local plaque effect could modify acid exposure without changing pigmentation; a melanocyte signaling effect could occur without changing calcium balance. Food-associated sugar, acidity, salt, oral hygiene, saliva and mucosal irritation are competing explanations. The framework does not recommend placing raw garlic or concentrated capsaicin on teeth or gums, or replacing established dental care.

### Age is not a uniform rewriting instruction

Chronological age can be associated with DNA methylation, chromatin accessibility, histone marks, transcriptional changes, senescence and changing cell composition. These processes differ by tissue, locus and exposure history. Epigenetic regulation changes gene use without necessarily changing DNA sequence; it does not create the inherited pathogenic variants listed in Section 1. Promoter methylation, enhancer methylation and gene-body methylation cannot all be interpreted as simple gene silencing.

Human skin studies identify age-associated methylation changes and distinguish intrinsic aging from chronic sun exposure. Other epidermal work finds substantial preservation of core developmental programs alongside localized regulatory changes. These results support a tissue-specific framework, not a predetermined direction of age-related methylation for AMELX, ENAM, MMP20, KLK4, FAM83H, SLC24A4, WDR72 or FAM20A. [32, 33]

### Pigment and enamel compartments require different models

In pigment cells, nominate MITF, TYR, DCT, TYRP1, MFSD12 and SLC24A4 as candidate loci, then measure methylation or accessibility, expression, protein abundance and pigment flux in the same cell type. These are proposed targets, not a claim that each locus has a validated age-dependent epigenetic trajectory. Human melanocyte senescence can affect nearby keratinocytes, showing that age effects may also be mediated through cell–cell signaling. [34]

For developmental enamel genes, the relevant window is enamel formation in ameloblasts. Mature erupted enamel is acellular and has no resident epigenome to rewrite. Adult age-associated oral changes must instead be studied in living salivary, mucosal, periodontal or other relevant cells and distinguished from accumulated wear, acid injury and pre-existing enamel defects. Blood or buccal methylation is not a validated substitute for historical ameloblast regulation.

## 9 Integrating the new variables into experiments

### A causal design rather than a list of associations

Treat chronological age, tissue-specific epigenetic state, allicin exposure and capsaicin exposure as distinct variables. Prespecify one primary outcome per experiment. Begin with separate dose–response studies and then test combinations in a factorial design; do not assume allicin and capsaicin are additive or synergistic. Add gravity or radiation conditions only after exposure and assay validity are established.

| Question | Required measurement | Critical control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Do compounds change pigment production? | Cysteine and glutathione status; chemically resolved pigment synthesis and loss; calcium transients. | Measured exposure, viability, vehicle, assay interference and pathway rescue. |
| Do compounds improve plaque function? | Multispecies viability, acidification, EPS, recovery and enamel mineral loss. | Saliva, substrate, food pH/sugar and exposure duration; nonpathogenic community members. |
| Does age alter pathway regulation? | Cell-resolved methylation or accessibility paired with RNA, protein and flux. | Donor, cell type, sun exposure, smoking, medications and culture passage. |
| Does an epigenetic change cause an effect? | Temporal ordering and targeted locus perturbation with functional rescue. | Matched genotype and non-targeting controls; distinguish expression from flux. |
| Does any effect matter in spaceflight? | Incremental calcium flux and oral outcomes beyond established mission factors. | Diet, exercise, hydration, renal function, exposure dose and repeated baseline data. |

For human exploratory analyses, model age continuously where appropriate and prespecify a small number of interaction terms, such as age by compound exposure. Use donor-level replication and repeated measures; cells and sequencing reads are not independent participants. Small astronaut cohorts are unsuitable for an unrestricted search across many genes, exposures and interactions.

### Epigenetic logic and falsification

An age association should first survive cell-composition adjustment and exposure control. A proposed regulatory mechanism then requires linkage from an epigenetic feature to gene expression or protein function, and finally to a measured pigment, microbial or calcium endpoint. DNA methylation clocks estimate an age-related signature; a clock shift alone does not establish reversal of pathway dysfunction.

Keep protein S-thioallylation and TRPV1 signaling distinct from epigenetic modification. They may affect downstream regulation, but such effects must be demonstrated. Reject a dietary pathway claim if the required concentration is not reached, the effect disappears after toxicity or pH controls, or the putative epigenetic mediator fails functional testing. A local oral benefit must not be reinterpreted automatically as correction of systemic calcium balance.
